# Scientific Editorial Style

## Purpose

PepEducation should be rigorous without sounding promotional, defensive, legalistic, or dismissive. Scientific uncertainty is explained as part of the evidence, not used as repetitive discouraging language.

## Core voice

- Evidence-led.
- Clear and useful.
- Calm and confident.
- Curious without exaggeration.
- Precise about evidence class and regulatory status.
- Accessible to non-specialists without diluting scientific meaning.

## Preferred framing

Use proportional language:

- Current evidence suggests...
- Early human studies indicate...
- Preclinical research has produced encouraging findings...
- Mechanistic research supports continued investigation...
- Human evidence remains limited, while laboratory findings provide a plausible research foundation...
- Additional controlled studies will help clarify durability, population boundaries and long-term safety...
- The strongest current signal appears in...
- Findings are most applicable to the population and conditions studied...

## Avoid as a default style

Do not repeatedly lead with:

- There is no evidence...
- This does not prove...
- Not proven...
- Insufficient evidence...
- No clinical benefit...

These phrases may be scientifically appropriate in specific contexts, but should not dominate the page. Explain what is known first, then define the boundary.

## Better examples

Instead of:

> GHK is endogenous and can bind copper. That does not prove that an administered preparation has a therapeutic benefit.

Prefer:

> GHK is an endogenous copper-binding tripeptide. Current research supports biological activity in matrix and tissue-remodeling models, while clinical outcomes remain dependent on formulation, exposure and the specific application studied.

Instead of:

> Animal findings cannot establish effectiveness in humans.

Prefer:

> Animal findings provide a translational signal and help define mechanisms, but human studies are needed to determine clinical magnitude, durability and safety.

## Evidence ordering

When possible, present sections in this order:

1. What the molecule or biomarker is.
2. Why researchers are interested.
3. Strongest observed evidence.
4. Evidence by class.
5. Safety and unresolved questions.
6. Regulatory status.
7. Scientific perspective.

## Evidence classes

Always distinguish:

- Human clinical evidence.
- Preclinical animal evidence.
- In vitro or ex vivo evidence.
- Mechanistic evidence.
- Theoretical interpretation.

Do not convert a mechanistic possibility into a clinical claim.

## Regulatory language

State regulatory status clearly and once in the appropriate section. Avoid turning every paragraph into a regulatory disclaimer.

Preferred:

> As of the stated review date, the compound remains investigational and has no authorized medicinal indication identified in the cited official databases.

## Safety language

Describe observed adverse events, study duration, population and missing data. Avoid implying safety from the absence of reported events.

Preferred:

> Available studies have not yet produced a dataset large or long enough to characterize uncommon, delayed or population-specific risks.

## Educational boundary

Use a concise platform-level statement:

> Educational content only. Not medical advice and not a substitute for qualified care.

Longer warnings should be reserved for contexts where a specific risk or ambiguity must be explained.

## Scientific Perspective component

Every major profile should conclude with a short synthesis containing:

- The strongest evidence signal.
- The most important limitation.
- The next research question.
- The practical meaning for an evidence-focused reader.

## Prohibited content

- Dosing or dose-escalation instructions.
- Reconstitution or injection instructions.
- Treatment cycles or stacks.
- Individualized treatment protocols.
- Product sourcing.
- Claims that investigational compounds are established as safe or effective.
- Fabricated references, trial identifiers, statistics or regulatory status.
